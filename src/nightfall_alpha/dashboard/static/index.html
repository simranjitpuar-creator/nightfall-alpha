Welcome to NightFall Alpha

A research desk for a simple idea: stocks that repeatedly gain overnight tend to keep doing it for a while.

Each trading day, the engine scores every stock in the universe by its recent overnight performance (last close to today's open), buys the strongest names at the close, sells them at the next close, and compounds the result after realistic transaction costs. This dashboard lets you run that strategy, compare it against benchmarks, test whether it survives out-of-sample, build optimized portfolios, and audit every single trade it would have made.

First-Run Quickstart

  1. Get data. Open System → Market Data, pick a source (Yahoo is fastest; Stooq has the longest history; Tiingo needs a free API key), leave tickers blank for the full S&P 500 universe, and press Download Real Data with Run backtest checked.
  2. Read the headline. The status bar at the top always shows ending equity, Sharpe, and max drawdown of the latest run.
  3. Check the curve. Signal Backtest shows the equity curve, drawdown, and today's candidate list. Hover any ? icon to learn what a control does before changing it.
  4. Stress it. In Evaluation → Walk-Forward, press Run Walk-Forward. If the edge only works in-sample, you will see it fall apart here.
  5. Audit it. In Trade Blotter, filter to Losers and scan for patterns — costs, specific symbols, specific eras.

Key Concepts in Plain English

Overnight return

Today's open ÷ yesterday's close − 1. The strategy only ever holds positions between one close and the next — it is never exposed during the trading day.

Signal rank

Each day every stock is scored by its rolling overnight Sharpe over the lookback window. Rank 1 is the strongest candidate; the book takes the top N.

Basis points (bps)

1 bp = 0.01%. Fees and slippage are charged per side in bps. Small numbers compound brutally over hundreds of thousands of trades, which is why cost assumptions matter.

Era costs

Trading in 1962 was far more expensive than today. The historical cost model charges era-appropriate bps so old performance is not flattered by modern commission rates.

Walk-forward

Instead of picking parameters on the full history, walk-forward fits them on a training window, tests on the following unseen window, then rolls forward. It is the main defense against overfitting.

Survivorship bias

Testing only on today's S&P 500 members ignores delisted failures and inflates results. The status bar badge tells you whether the universe is point-in-time or survivor-skewed.

Capacity

A strategy trading $1,000 behaves nothing like one trading $1B. Capacity limits model how much capital the overnight book could actually deploy before moving prices.

Benchmarks

Each benchmark series starts at $1.00 on its first available date inside the strategy window. Gaps mean the data does not exist yet (MSCI World's URTH proxy starts in 2012) — never a flat zero.

Read This Before Trusting Any Number

  • This is a research tool, not investment advice. Past overnight patterns may stop working at any time.
  • Sample data is synthetic. If you have not downloaded real data, results come from generated prices and are useful only for learning the interface. The Market Data tab fixes this.
  • Fills are idealized. The backtest assumes you can buy at the close and sell at the next close at size. Real overnight auctions have spreads, partial fills, and gaps.
  • Benchmarks are proxies. MSCI World is approximated by the URTH ETF (from 2012) and the 10Y Treasury by a yield-derived return — useful for context, not for accounting.
  • Survivorship status matters. Check the badge in the status bar. A survivor-skewed universe flatters every metric on this dashboard.

Two Suites, Two Different Questions

read this before comparing numbers across this tab
  • Allocation suites (Builder above, Saved Signal Universe below): a fixed basket of names with no daily stock selection. The optimizers only decide how much of each name to hold — estimated once over the lookback window (Static Weights, then constant-mix rebalanced back to target daily) or re-estimated at each Monthly/Quarterly/Annual rebalance date. Membership never changes; no signal is involved.
  • Signal-Optimized Suite (bottom of this tab): the strategy you actually trade. Every night the universe is re-ranked exactly like the Signal Backtest, the top-N candidates are selected, and the optimizer you choose sizes that night's book only, estimated on the trailing overnight returns of those names. Membership and weights change daily, and every weight change is billed at each stock's own cost rate.

Comparing the two: the allocation suites answer "what was the best way to hold this basket over the window?" — the signal-optimized suite answers "what would nightly signal trading with optimizer sizing have done?" Different questions, different numbers.

Sources for the Cost Estimates

research-grade approximations, not vendor data
  • Pre-1975 fixed commissions & 1/8 spreads: NYSE fixed-commission schedule and fractional (eighth) quoting — see NYSE fact histories and SEC accounts of the fixed-rate era.
  • May 1, 1975 ("May Day"): SEC-mandated end of fixed commissions; negotiated rates begin. Source: SEC historical releases on the Securities Acts Amendments of 1975.
  • 1990s discount brokers: Schwab-era commission compression; academic surveys of retail trading costs (e.g. studies of effective spreads on NYSE/Nasdaq through the 1990s).
  • 1997 order-handling rules: SEC's Limit Order Display and ECN rules; quoting moves to sixteenths. Source: SEC rule releases 34-37619A / 34-37619.
  • 2001 decimalization: NYSE/Nasdaq move from fractions to pennies (phased Jan–Apr 2001). Source: SEC decimal-pricing phase-in schedule.
  • 2005–2007 Reg NMS: Regulation NMS (2005, phased through 2007) plus ECN fragmentation drive sub-penny effective spreads. Source: SEC Reg NMS releases; academic spread literature.
  • 2015+ near-zero commissions: zero-commission retail trading becomes standard (2019 industry-wide); penny spreads on liquid names. Source: major broker pricing schedules.

Exact bps figures are our approximations of round-trip reality for a liquid large-cap book in each period. Override any era in config/settings.yml if you have better estimates — the study re-runs from those numbers.

Dashboard Framework

Methodology, data lineage, formulas, and model limitations

NightFall Alpha is a research dashboard for testing an overnight-effect equity strategy and related portfolio construction methods. The core loop is: collect daily OHLCV data, calculate close-to-next-open returns, rank stocks by rolling overnight behavior, backtest a daily overnight book, then compare portfolio optimizers on the same return data.

The dashboard is a research tool, not a live execution system. Results depend on data quality, symbol coverage, point-in-time assumptions, transaction cost assumptions, and the exact universe selected for each run.

System Map

  • Overview is the landing tab: what the strategy does, the live state of the loaded data, the five-step workflow, a first-run quickstart, and a plain-English glossary.
  • Market Data updates the local price cache at data/processed/prices.parquet.
  • Signal Backtest reads the local price cache, creates overnight features, ranks stocks, runs the strategy, and writes reports to data/reports.
  • Evaluation compares the strategy against benchmarks and runs walk-forward out-of-sample tests.
  • Portfolio Research can either build a fresh optimizer suite from the current builder form or display the saved universe suite from the latest signal/data pipeline run.
  • Trade Blotter reads the saved signal backtest executions from data/reports/trades.csv.
  • Help icons (?) on controls and table headers explain what each item is, how it affects the model, and practical usage details.

Return Definitions

  • Overnight return is calculated as next open divided by prior close minus one. This is the main strategy return.
  • Close-to-close return is calculated as current close divided by prior close minus one. This is available in Portfolio Research for ordinary daily portfolio testing.
  • Signal date is the date on which the model forms the overnight book. Exit date is the next available open used to realize the return.

Market Data Methodology

Sources

  • Yahoo Finance uses the Python yfinance library for adjusted daily OHLCV data. It is usually the fastest source for current US equities.
  • Yahoo Max History uses the same provider but requests the longest history available from Yahoo. The actual first date still depends on each ticker.
  • Stooq Long History uses Stooq daily files through the app's Stooq provider. US symbols are normalized into Stooq's expected format where needed.
  • Tiingo uses Tiingo's official free EOD API (free API key, 500 requests/day). Set TIINGO_API_KEY in your environment or .env file before using it.
  • Incremental downloads only fetch bars after each symbol's last cached date (with a small overlap), so daily refreshes avoid re-downloading full history.
  • S&P 500 universe metadata can come from the public S&P 500 table on Wikipedia. Wikipedia is used for constituent names/sectors, not for price bars.

Inputs

  • Source selects the provider used for a download. Changing it does not change saved data until a download runs.
  • Tickers restricts the request to typed symbols. If blank, the app uses the S&P 500 universe list.
  • Start and End define the requested date range. Providers can return less history than requested.
  • Limit caps how many universe symbols are requested when Tickers is blank. It is ignored for typed ticker lists.
  • Run backtest reruns the Signal Backtest immediately after the download and passes the new symbols/date window into the signal engine.

Cache And Updates

  • Downloaded prices are merged into data/processed/prices.parquet and de-duplicated by date and symbol.
  • The app does not download all market data on startup. A refresh happens when you use the Market Data tab, or when Portfolio Research fills missing symbols or refreshes history.
  • The cache loader repairs malformed rows, removes impossible future dates, normalizes symbols, coerces numeric fields, and rewrites the cleaned file when needed.
  • Provider failures, delisted symbols, renamed symbols, missing opens, and stale bars can create gaps. The app filters unusable rows, but it cannot guarantee institutional point-in-time data quality.

Historical Data Depth: The 1962 Floor

why backtests cannot start before 1962 on the default setup
  • Yahoo Finance adjusted daily history starts 1962-01-02 for most long-listed US tickers (IBM, XOM, MO, and others all begin exactly that day). The companies traded earlier; Yahoo simply does not carry older bars. This is the binding constraint on the backtest start date.
  • The Price Start control filters the local cache — it cannot create history. Selecting a date earlier than the cache's first bar silently clamps the run to the first available bar; the Signal Backtest summary line and the Overview data note now state the clamp explicitly when it happens.
  • Stooq Long History is currently unreliable for automation. Stooq presents a JavaScript proof-of-work browser check to non-browser clients, so programmatic downloads can return a verification page instead of prices. When this happens the app reports the per-symbol errors and recommends Yahoo or Tiingo.
  • Tiingo (free API key) is a dependable alternative but typically reaches back to the 1990s per ticker — deeper than nothing, not deeper than Yahoo's floor for the oldest names.
  • Pre-1962 broad US equity history is effectively unavailable for free in automatable form. Paid vendors (CRSP, Compustat) are the realistic route; 1926+ index-level series exist (e.g. via academic sources) but not per-stock OHLCV for a 500-name universe.
  • Practical implication: treat 1962 as the hard start of trustworthy full-universe results in this project. Any finding about earlier decades would rest on a thin, expensive-to-acquire dataset.

Signal Backtest Methodology

Strategy Logic

  • The signal engine builds overnight features for every symbol with enough valid history.
  • For each date, it estimates each stock's rolling overnight mean, volatility, win rate, and Sharpe-like score over the selected lookback window.
  • Stocks are ranked by the overnight score. The top ranked names that pass the minimum signal threshold become the current overnight book.
  • Each selected stock receives a target weight subject to the Max Weight cap. Unused capital remains implicit cash.
  • The backtest applies the next close-to-open return to each target weight, subtracts transaction costs, compounds equity, and records one trade row per symbol per signal date.

Inputs

  • Initial Equity is the starting capital used for equity, notional, gross PnL, net PnL, and ending equity. It does not change percentage returns or rankings.
  • Fees Bps is the assumed commission or explicit trading fee in basis points per side.
  • Slippage Bps is the assumed execution slippage in basis points per side.
  • Signal Lookback controls the rolling window used to estimate overnight score. Shorter windows react faster and are noisier; longer windows are more stable and slower.
  • Min History is the minimum valid observations required before a symbol is eligible. Higher values reduce thin-history noise.
  • Top N controls how many highest-ranked names are selected per signal date.
  • Max Weight caps any single signal candidate's target weight.
  • Min Signal filters out names whose signal score is too low, even if they rank highly relative to the rest of the universe.
  • Price Start, Price End, and Price Tickers restrict the data window and symbols used in that signal run.

Transaction Cost Formula

For the Signal Backtest, cost is applied every signal date because the strategy is modeled as an overnight trade book. The per-side rate is (Fees Bps + Slippage Bps) / 10000. Daily cost return is 2 * gross exposure * per-side rate, because the book enters and exits. Net return is gross return - cost return.

Era Cost Model (Historical)

With the historical cost model, the flat fee/slippage inputs are replaced by a step function of set rates per era — not yearly averages, and not interpolated values. Each trade date pays the rate of the latest era whose start is on or before that date, per side, on both entry and exit. The eras follow real US market-structure events:

  • 1900–Apr 1975 — 90 bps/side (30 fees + 60 slippage): fixed NYSE commissions plus wide eighth-spreads.
  • May 1975–1989 — 55 bps/side (15 + 40): "May Day" ends fixed commissions; negotiated rates begin.
  • 1990–1996 — 33 bps/side (8 + 25): discount brokers compress commissions; spreads shrink.
  • 1997–Apr 2001 — 20 bps/side (5 + 15): SEC order-handling rules; sixteenth-spreads; ECNs emerge.
  • Apr 2001–2006 — 10 bps/side (2 + 8): decimalization replaces fractions with pennies.
  • 2007–2014 — 4 bps/side (1 + 3): Reg NMS and ECN fragmentation produce sub-penny effective spreads.
  • 2015–present — 1.5 bps/side (0.5 + 1): near-zero retail commissions; penny spreads on liquid names.

Because the overnight book turns over roughly 100% per day, the round-trip drag is about twice the per-side rate: ~1.8% per day in 1962 versus ~0.03% today. Over a 64-year backtest this difference is decisive — a strategy that looks brilliant at flat modern costs can be mathematically impossible at 1960s costs. The Research Findings tab quantifies exactly when the edge starts surviving its costs, and every era's numbers can be overridden in config/settings.yml.

Curves

  • Equity curve shows compounded account value after each realized overnight book. It reflects transaction costs and the Initial Equity input.
  • Drawdown curve shows percentage decline from the previous equity peak. Deeper or longer drawdowns mean the strategy required more patience and risk tolerance.
  • Range buttons only zoom the displayed curve. They do not recalculate signals or metrics.

Current Overnight Candidates

  • Entry is the signal date. Exit is the next open date used for realized return.
  • Rank is the daily ordering by the overnight score.
  • Ticker, Name, Sector, ETF, Market Cap come from universe metadata and help interpret concentration.
  • Target Weight is the model's allocation to that symbol after applying Top N and Max Weight.
  • O/N Sharpe, O/N Mean, O/N Vol, and Lookback Win are rolling overnight statistics used to understand why a candidate was selected.
  • Next O/N Return is the realized next overnight return for backtest accounting. In live use, this would not be known at signal time.

Visible Metrics

  • Initial Equity and Ending Equity show starting and final compounded capital.
  • Total Return is ending equity divided by initial equity minus one.
  • CAGR annualizes compounded return using elapsed calendar years.
  • Annual Vol annualizes the standard deviation of daily net returns.
  • Sharpe is annualized excess return divided by annualized volatility.
  • Sortino is annualized excess return divided by downside deviation.
  • Calmar is CAGR divided by absolute max drawdown.
  • 1Y, 3Y, and 5Y rolling Sharpe, Sortino, and Calmar recalculate those ratios on the most recent trailing windows.
  • Max DD is the worst percentage drop from an equity peak.
  • Win Rate is the share of daily net returns above zero.
  • Profit Factor is the sum of positive daily net returns divided by the absolute sum of negative daily net returns.
  • VaR 95 is the historical fifth percentile of daily net returns. CVaR 95 is the average of returns at or below that threshold.
  • Cost Drag is the sum of modeled transaction cost returns over the test period.
  • Avg Exposure is the average absolute portfolio exposure used each signal date.
  • Avg Turnover is the average modeled round-trip turnover. In the Signal Backtest this is typically two times gross exposure because each book enters and exits overnight.

Portfolio Research Methodology

Builder Inputs

  • Universe Mode selects typed stocks or the entire local price universe.
  • Missing Data chooses the provider used only when missing symbols need to be filled or Refresh History is enabled.
  • Tickers and Universe Selector define the selected-stock investable universe.
  • Return Model chooses overnight returns or close-to-close returns for the optimizer matrix.
  • Initial Equity scales dollar outputs. It does not change weights or percentage returns.
  • Fees Bps and Slippage Bps set portfolio research transaction cost assumptions.
  • Default Max Weight caps single-name concentration unless an optimizer-specific cap overrides it.
  • Rebalance chooses static weights, monthly, quarterly, or annual re-optimization.
  • Lookback is the trailing estimation window when Use All History is off. In rebalance mode it is applied at each rebalance using only prior data.
  • Use All History uses every available eligible observation. In rebalance mode this becomes expanding prior history.
  • Data Since affects provider refresh requests. It does not trim the optimizer if no download occurs.
  • Auto-fill Missing downloads missing selected symbols. Refresh History intentionally refreshes selected symbols from the chosen provider.

Portfolio Cost Formula

Portfolio Research models costs as allocation turnover, not as a daily overnight liquidation book. The per-side rate is (Fees Bps + Slippage Bps) / 10000 per stock: fees are uniform across names, while each stock's slippage is scaled by its relative daily volatility (clipped to 0.25x–4x the cross-sectional mean) — volatile names cost more to trade, calm names less, and the average stock pays roughly the input rate. Static portfolios pay initial allocation turnover on the first row. Rebalanced portfolios pay turnover when target weights change at each rebalance. Because fixed weights are applied to daily returns, every day also implies drift rebalancing — trimming winners and topping up losers back to target — and those daily sales and purchases are billed at each stock's own per-side rate. Net return is gross portfolio return - turnover cost.

Optimizers

  • Kelly 50% estimates raw Kelly weights from inverse covariance times expected return, clips long-only negative bets to zero, then scales by the Kelly fraction. It is meant for growth-seeking allocation but is sensitive to noisy expected returns.
  • Mean Variance maximizes expected return minus a variance penalty: mu' w - 0.5 * lambda * w' Sigma w, with long-only weights and caps. Higher risk aversion makes variance more expensive.
  • Minimum Variance ignores expected return and searches for the lowest-volatility long-only capped portfolio. It is useful as a defensive risk baseline.
  • Inverse Volatility allocates more to lower-volatility stocks using inverse standard deviation, then applies caps. It is simple, transparent, and less dependent on expected-return estimates.
  • CVaR Aware focuses on the historical loss tail. The confidence input controls how extreme the tail sample is. It is relevant when downside concentration matters more than ordinary variance.
  • Black-Litterman builds a prior return estimate from covariance and equal market weights, then allocates with a mean-variance objective. In this app it is a simplified no-view Black-Litterman-style stabilizer, not a full institutional view model.

Portfolio Metrics

  • Start, End, Years, Obs describe the performance sample used for each optimizer row.
  • Initial Equity, Ending Equity, Period Return, CAGR, Ann Return describe compounded return after costs.
  • Annual Vol, Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, Max DD, VaR 95, CVaR 95 describe volatility, downside, drawdown, and tail risk after costs.
  • Cost Drag is the sum of modeled portfolio allocation, rebalance, and daily drift-rebalancing cost returns.
  • Rolling ratios show the most recent 1, 3, and 5 year risk-adjusted performance windows when enough observations exist.
  • Win Rate and Profit Factor summarize daily net return consistency.
  • Invested is the sum of target weights. Cash is uninvested capital left by caps or conservative methods.
  • Cap is the max-weight limit applied to that optimizer. Max Weight is the largest actual position.
  • Rebalances counts portfolio re-optimization snapshots. Avg Turnover is the average absolute weight change at rebalance events.
  • Positions is the number of non-zero target weights.

Trade Blotter Methodology

Data Source

The Trade Blotter is linked to the latest saved Signal Backtest, not to the Portfolio Builder. It reads data/reports/trades.csv, which is written whenever the signal research pipeline runs.

Summary Outputs

  • Source Trades is the full number of saved signal execution rows before filters.
  • Filtered Trades is the count after symbol, date, and PnL filters.
  • Loaded Rows is the number currently displayed in the browser table.
  • Symbols counts unique filtered tickers.
  • Source Start and Source End show the full saved blotter date range.
  • Latest Exit and Latest Book Trades describe the most recent saved overnight book.
  • Win Rate is the share of filtered trade rows with positive net PnL.
  • Total Net PnL, Avg Trade PnL, Best Trade, and Worst Trade summarize trade-level dollars after costs.
  • Total Costs is the dollar sum of fees and slippage allocated to filtered trade rows.
  • Avg Weight is the average absolute target weight of filtered trade rows.

Execution Rows

  • Entry Date is the signal date. Exit Date is the next open date used for return realization.
  • Rank is the daily signal rank.
  • Ticker is the traded symbol.
  • Target Weight is the model allocation to that ticker for the overnight book.
  • Overnight Return is the realized close-to-next-open return.
  • Notional is starting equity times target weight.
  • Gross PnL is notional times overnight return before costs.
  • Cost is that row's allocation of the daily round-trip fee and slippage model.
  • Net PnL is gross PnL minus cost.
  • O/N Sharpe is the rolling overnight score used in ranking.

Limitations And Model Risk

  • Survivorship bias: the S&P 500 universe is based on available/current metadata, not a perfect historical point-in-time membership file.
  • Data quality: free data providers can revise bars, omit delisted names, change adjustment methods, throttle requests, or return incomplete histories.
  • Opening print realism: the model assumes the next open is achievable. Real fills can differ because of liquidity, spreads, auctions, halts, and order handling.
  • Transaction costs are simplified: fees and slippage are basis-point assumptions. The app does not model borrow, taxes, exchange fees by venue, market impact, partial fills, or order queue priority.
  • No live trading controls yet: there is no broker risk gate, order staging, kill switch, position reconciliation, or regulatory compliance layer in this dashboard.
  • Optimizer sensitivity: expected returns, covariance, CVaR, and Black-Litterman-style priors can be unstable, especially with short lookbacks or sparse symbols.
  • Overfitting risk: tuning lookbacks, Top N, caps, cost assumptions, and optimizer settings on the same history can make results look better than future performance.
  • Regime changes: overnight effects can decay, invert, or concentrate in specific sectors and volatility regimes.
  • Interpretation: backtests are research estimates. They should be validated out of sample and compared against simple benchmarks before any capital is committed.
